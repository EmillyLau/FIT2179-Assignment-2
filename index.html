Section 1

Where Do Floods Strike
Most Often?

Flood impacts are not distributed evenly across Malaysia. Some states experience recurring flood events and large economic losses, while others face less frequent but more localised impacts.

Map evidence

The East Coast Pays the Highest Price

Not all states suffer equally. Kelantan and Terengganu record over RM 960 in flood losses per household, nearly 20 times higher than Selangor, despite Selangor having far more homes.

The map encodes total losses per living quarter, a fairer measure than raw totals. It reveals that geographic location, not population size, determines flood exposure in Malaysia.

Kelantan records the highest loss per household nationwide, driven by its position in the Northeast Monsoon corridor, not by economic size.
Timeline evidence

2021 Was Catastrophic. But the Pattern Repeats Every Year.

Each circle marks a state affected in that year. Bigger circles mean more people displaced. Darker circles mean more flood events hit that state.

2021 stands out immediately. The largest circles appear across Kelantan, Pahang, and Selangor at the same time. But look closer: the northeast states show circles in almost every year. This is not an occasional disaster. This is a chronic, annual reality.

Kelantan and Terengganu appear in nearly every column, confirming these states face flood displacement almost every year.
Section 2

What Drives Flooding
in Malaysia?

Floods do not happen in isolation. They are driven by climate patterns decades in the making: intensifying rainfall, rising temperatures, and monsoon systems that follow a predictable but devastating calendar.

Long-term climate

Malaysia Is Getting Hotter and Wetter

Average annual rainfall across all weather stations has risen since 2000, with sharp spikes in 2014 and 2021, both catastrophic flood years.

Maximum temperatures show a steady upward trend. A warmer atmosphere holds more moisture, making extreme rainfall events more likely and more intense. Drag the overview bar at the bottom to zoom into any time period.

The dashed trend lines confirm both rainfall and temperature are rising: the two drivers that amplify flood risk.
Rainfall anomaly

The Worst Flood Years Were Also the Wettest Years

Each blue bar shows how far that year's rainfall exceeded the long-term average. Each brown bar shows a drier-than-normal year.

The pattern is clear: every major flood disaster aligns with a strongly positive anomaly. 2021 recorded the largest deviation on record.

2021's rainfall anomaly was the highest in 22 years of data, confirming the December floods were statistically extreme.
Seasonal pattern

The Northeast Monsoon Floods the Same States, Every November

Each cell shows how many flood events struck a state in that month, across 2011-2025. States are grouped geographically: east coast at the top, west coast in the middle, East Malaysia at the bottom.

A dark band appears in November-January for Kelantan, Terengganu, and Pahang: the Northeast Monsoon corridor. Click a colour in the legend to highlight that frequency band.

No other month comes close. November-January is Malaysia's flood season, and the east coast bears almost all of it.
Section 3

How Do Different Floods Behave?

Floods may share the same name, but they behave very differently. Flash floods, general floods, and riverine floods each have distinct durations, geographic footprints, and patterns of damage. Understanding these differences matters for how communities prepare and recover.

Duration by type

Flash Floods Last Longer Than You'd Expect.

Flash floods in Malaysia typically last around 8 days — far longer than the brief urban flash floods seen elsewhere. This reflects how Malaysia's flash flood events often affect large river catchments, not just city streets.

General floods have the shortest typical duration — around 4 days — but with the widest spread, meaning some events linger much longer than expected. Riverine floods sit in the middle, with consistent duration around 6–7 days.

The longest event lasted 28 days: a December 2016 general flood across Kelantan, Terengganu, and six other states.
Section 4

What Are the Human and Economic Consequences?

Behind the rainfall statistics lies a human reality: homes damaged, crops destroyed, roads and bridges washed away. This section quantifies the burden and asks what explains the scale of losses.

Loss ranking

Kelantan Lost RM 351 Million. What Was Damaged?

The chart ranks states by total flood losses (RM) across 2024 and 2025. Click any state to see exactly what was damaged: living quarters, vehicles, businesses, agriculture, or infrastructure.

Infrastructure losses dominate most states. But Kedah is the exception: agriculture losses drove most of its 2024 damage, reflecting its role as Malaysia's rice bowl.

Kedah's agriculture losses in 2024 exceeded its infrastructure losses: the only state where farmland damage outweighed roads and bridges.
Rainfall versus loss

More Rain Does Not Mean More Damage

Each point is one Malaysian state, plotted by average annual rainfall against total flood losses. If rainfall alone drove damage, all points would follow the trend line closely.

They do not. Sarawak receives Malaysia's heaviest rainfall yet records moderate losses: its sparse population absorbs impact. Kelantan receives less rain but loses far more. Monsoon intensity and infrastructure density amplify damage beyond rainfall alone.

States above the trend line suffer disproportionate losses, showing urban density and land use matter as much as rainfall totals.
Section 5

Which States Face the Greatest Risk?

The previous sections examined floods through geography, climate, behaviour, and economic impact. This final chart combines those dimensions into one vulnerability profile, revealing which states face the greatest overall risk.

Composite index

Kelantan and Terengganu Top Every Dimension

Each bar represents one state, scored across four normalised dimensions: flood losses, people affected, flood frequency, and rainfall intensity. Longer bars mean higher vulnerability.

Hover for the exact score on each dimension. Kelantan scores highest, topping both economic losses and rainfall intensity. Terengganu follows closely. Putrajaya and Kuala Lumpur score low, not because they never flood, but because losses are modest relative to their scale.

Malaysia's flood crisis is above all a northeast coast crisis, driven by geography, monsoon exposure, and underinvestment in flood resilience.
Key takeaways

Damage
