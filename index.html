When the Rain Won't Stop: Flood Risk & Climate Vulnerability in Malaysia

This dashboard follows one central question: when floods arrive, which Malaysian communities carry the heaviest burden? The answer is not found in rainfall alone.

Read from left to right and top to bottom: the story moves from where floods happen, to why the climate signal matters, to who suffers the greatest losses.

Section 1 · Geographic Overview

Where Do Floods Strike Hardest?

Start with place. The first pattern is spatial: the east coast repeatedly stands out, especially when losses are adjusted for households rather than shown only as raw totals.

Not all states suffer equally. Kelantan and Terengganu face the full force of the monsoon, recording the highest losses per household.

Geography, not population size, determines flood exposure. The map below reveals east coast states clustered in deeper red.

Loss per household reveals the true burden

Total flood losses (RM) divided by residential units per state. Kelantan and Terengganu absorbed the most damage relative to households.

Labuan and Putrajaya have no household count data and are shown in grey.

  • The east coast pattern suggests household exposure is concentrated in states repeatedly hit by monsoon flooding.
  • Loss per household makes smaller states' vulnerability visible instead of letting population size dominate the story.

The household map shows vulnerability, but it does not show disruption. The next map adds a human lens by asking how many people had to move when flood events were recorded.

Where did floods hit — and how many people were displaced?

Displacement is not evenly distributed. Repeatedly affected states show how flood risk becomes a recurring disruption rather than a one-off emergency.

Section 2 · Climate & Seasonality

A Wetter, Hotter Malaysia

Flood risk is not only about one extreme storm. The climate charts show the background conditions: rainfall is variable, heat is rising, and flood months follow a clear monsoon rhythm.

Malaysia's flood risk is not static. Rainfall is growing more variable, temperatures are rising, and monsoon floods are intensifying.

Flooding follows a seasonal rhythm. The Northeast Monsoon affects Kelantan, Terengganu, and Pahang.

Rainfall and heat are both trending upward

Average annual rainfall shows strong year-to-year fluctuation, while maximum temperatures show a clearer upward trend.

  • The 2014 and 2021 flood years sit within a climate record marked by rainfall swings and steadily rising heat.
  • Temperature rises more consistently than rainfall, adding background stress even when annual rainfall fluctuates.

The monsoon belt floods like clockwork

A dark band appears in November–January for Kelantan, Terengganu, and Pahang.

Sabah and Sarawak show floods spread more evenly across the year.

  • Seasonality is strongest on the peninsula's east coast.
  • Borneo's less concentrated pattern suggests broader year-round exposure rather than a single dominant flood season.

The seasonal pattern explains when floods are likely, but anomaly years explain why some seasons become disasters. The bars below separate ordinary wet years from unusually wet years.

Above-average rainfall years were the flood years

Each bar shows how far annual rainfall deviated from the long-term mean. Wet anomalies align closely with major flood seasons.

Section 3 · Human & Economic Impact

The Toll: Lives, Livelihoods, and Losses

The story now shifts from hazard to consequence. Floods become a social and economic crisis when homes, farms, roads, businesses, and lives are placed in the path of water.

Kelantan and Terengganu lead total losses by a wide margin

States ranked by total flood losses (RM), 2024–2025. The sector breakdown shows whether damage is concentrated in homes, infrastructure, agriculture, or business activity.

Large losses are not simply a rainfall ranking. The scatterplot tests that assumption directly by comparing average rainfall with recorded economic damage.

Rainfall alone does not predict losses

States above the trend line suffer disproportionate losses relative to rainfall. Urban exposure can amplify damage.

  • Some states lose more than their rainfall levels would suggest, pointing to the role of exposure, settlement patterns, and assets.
  • The labelled outliers show that vulnerability is shaped by what sits in the floodplain, not only by how much rain falls.

Flood duration changes the kind of response needed

Some flood types are short, sharp disruptions, while others last long enough to strain evacuation centres, transport links, and household recovery.

  • Longer flood durations increase the pressure on relief logistics and public services.
  • Variation within each flood type shows why preparedness plans need to account for both typical events and outliers.

Section 4 · Composite Vulnerability

Which States Are Most at Risk?

The final view brings the earlier evidence together. It should not be read as a prediction model; it is a transparent summary of the observed signals that repeatedly appeared throughout the dashboard.

No single number captures flood vulnerability. The final synthesis combines rainfall, flood frequency, losses per household, and affected population.

Kelantan and Terengganu remain the clearest east coast priorities, while Johor rises in the composite ranking because many people are affected there.

A composite view shows three priority states

The radial chart brings the dashboard's main signals into one final comparison. Kelantan and Terengganu remain prominent through losses and rainfall, while Johor stands out through affected population and event frequency.

  • States with high losses and high affected populations require both infrastructure protection and social support planning.
  • The synthesis helps separate broad climate exposure from places where people and assets are most at risk.

The conclusion is consistent across the dashboard: flood vulnerability is highest where physical exposure and social disruption overlap. Flood resilience planning should therefore prioritise east coast household losses while also recognising high-displacement states such as Johor.
